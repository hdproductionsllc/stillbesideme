The Rainbow Bridge Poem for Dogs

The full poem, the true story of who wrote it, and a free printable version to keep, frame, or share. Written for dogs and every beloved animal who ever waited by the door.

If you have just lost a dog, the Rainbow Bridge may be the first thing a friend sends you, the words a vet clinic tucks into a condolence card, or the poem you find at 2 a.m. when the house is too quiet. There is a reason it has comforted millions of grieving pet owners: it takes the one fear that haunts every one of us – are they alone, are they scared, are they waiting for me? – and answers it gently. They are safe. They are happy. And yes, they are waiting.

Below is the complete poem, followed by the real story of where it came from and how people use it today.

The Rainbow Bridge Poem (Full Version)

Just this side of heaven is a place called Rainbow Bridge.

When an animal dies that has been especially close to someone here, that pet goes to Rainbow Bridge. There are meadows and hills for all of our special friends so they can run and play together. There is plenty of food, water and sunshine, and our friends are warm and comfortable.

All the animals who had been ill and old are restored to health and vigor. Those who were hurt or maimed are made whole and strong again, just as we remember them in our dreams of days and times gone by.

The animals are happy and content, except for one small thing; they each miss someone very special to them, who had to be left behind.

They all run and play together, but the day comes when one suddenly stops and looks into the distance. His bright eyes are intent. His eager body quivers. Suddenly he begins to run from the group, flying over the green grass, his legs carrying him faster and faster.

You have been spotted, and when you and your special friend finally meet, you cling together in joyous reunion, never to be parted again. The happy kisses rain upon your face; your hands again caress the beloved head, and you look once more into the trusting eyes of your pet, so long gone from your life but never absent from your heart.

Then you cross Rainbow Bridge together.

Who Wrote the Rainbow Bridge Poem?

For most of its life, the Rainbow Bridge had no known author. It passed from person to person – photocopied, forwarded, pinned to bulletin boards in veterinary offices – and spread most widely from the 1980s and 1990s onward, when pet-loss support was becoming something people talked about openly. Over the years it was attributed to several different writers, and for a long time no one could say for certain who first wrote it.

That changed in 2023. Research made public that year credited Edna Clyne-Rekhy, a Scottish woman who said she wrote the piece in 1959, at nineteen years old, after the death of her dog Major. According to that account, she wrote it in a single sitting to comfort herself and never sought credit, which is why the poem drifted for decades as an anonymous work of consolation. It is the most widely accepted attribution today. We share it here because the story behind these words is as tender as the words themselves – a teenager’s private grief that quietly went on to comfort the world.

Is the Rainbow Bridge Only for Dogs?

No. The poem is written for all animals who have been “especially close to someone.” It is searched for and shared most often after the loss of a dog, which is why you likely found this page – but it belongs just as fully to the cat who claimed the warmest spot in the house, the horse who knew your voice, the rabbit, the bird, the small companion who made a life feel less lonely. How People Use the Rainbow Bridge Poem

Because the imagery is vivid and comforting without being abstract, the Rainbow Bridge works in almost any memorial setting:

In a sympathy card. When you don’t know what to say to someone who just lost their dog, the poem says it for you. Pair it with one specific memory of their pet, and it becomes deeply personal. If you’d like help finding the right words, try our free sympathy message helper.

Framed with a photo. Printed beside a picture of your dog, the Rainbow Bridge becomes a daily point of gentle contact – the kind that reminds you of the love before it reminds you of the loss.

To explain loss to a child. The meadow, the play, the reunion – these are images a child can hold. Many parents read the Rainbow Bridge aloud as a way to talk about where a pet has gone.

At a farewell or graveside. Read slowly, it gives a small ceremony its shape. It marks the moment as something that mattered.

Make It About Your Dog, Not Every Dog

Here is the honest limitation of even the most beloved poem: the Rainbow Bridge was written for everyone, which means it was written for no one in particular. It doesn’t mention the way your dog leaned his whole weight against you for a hug, or the ridiculous fire-hydrant inspection on every single walk, or the sound he made when he heard your car in the driveway. Those specific things are what made your dog yours.
